# PART A — ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

---

**Q1. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* LLMs are neural networks — specifically transformer-based models — trained on massive text datasets to predict the next most probable token given a sequence. They are not databases (A), keyword matchers (B), or search engines (D), though they can be augmented with search tools.

---

**Q2. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* A token is the fundamental unit of text processing in LLMs. In English, a token is roughly ¾ of a word on average — common words like "the" are one token, while longer words may split into 2–3 tokens. Tokens are NOT individual characters (C) or complete sentences (D).

---

**Q3. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* The context window is the total number of tokens the model can "see" at once — it includes both your input (prompt) and the model's output. Crucially, it is NOT permanent memory (D) — when a new conversation starts, the model has no memory of previous chats.

---

**Q4. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* Temperature = 0 means the model always picks the single highest-probability next token, making outputs deterministic and consistent. This is ideal for factual tasks, classification, and code. High temperature (e.g., 0.9) introduces randomness for creative tasks. Temperature does not affect speed (A).

---

**Q5. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* The correct hierarchy is: Artificial Intelligence (broadest) → Machine Learning (a subset of AI) → Deep Learning (a subset of ML using neural networks) → LLMs (a specific type of deep learning model). This is a common knowledge structure question tested in Module 1, Session 1.

---

**Q6. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* CRAFT = **C**ontext, **R**ole, **A**ction, **F**ormat, **T**one. Role defines the identity, expertise, or perspective you assign to the AI. Without a Role, the AI defaults to a generic assistant persona, which may not produce domain-appropriate output.

---

**Q7. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* Role is the "who" — it defines the persona of the AI for this interaction (e.g., "You are a senior marketing strategist"). Context provides background information. Task defines what to do. Format defines how to structure the output.

---

**Q8. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* Vague outputs almost always trace back to vague prompts. If the Task ("what to do") is unclear or the Context ("why and for whom") is missing, the AI fills the gap with generalities. The model generates the most statistically probable response to a vague instruction — which is a vague answer. Temperature (C) affects creativity, not specificity. Prompt length (D) alone doesn't determine quality.

---

**Q9. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* Hallucination is one of the most important limitations to understand: LLMs are trained to generate statistically plausible text, not factually verified text. They can produce confidently-stated false information (wrong dates, invented citations, fabricated statistics). This is why verification of all factual claims is mandatory in professional use.

---

**Q10. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* `max_tokens` directly limits the length of the generated response. Temperature (A) controls randomness. Top-p (B) controls token selection diversity. Frequency penalty (D) discourages word repetition in the output.

---

**Q11. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* Format specification is one of the highest-ROI prompt elements. By specifying "respond in a table with columns X, Y, Z" or "use numbered steps," you get output that can be directly inserted into a document without reformatting. It does not prevent hallucination (D) or enforce formality (B).

---

**Q12. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* A negative instruction explicitly tells the AI what NOT to do. "Do not use bullet points" is a constraint against a common AI default (using lists for everything). Negative instructions are valuable when the model has a strong tendency to default to a format or style you don't want.

---

**Q13. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* Iterative refinement is systematic — it requires (1) diagnosing WHY the output failed (too generic? wrong format? wrong tone?), then (2) making a specific change to address that root cause. Simply re-running the same prompt (A) is not iteration — it's hoping for luck.

---

**Q14. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* "Tell me about marketing" has a task (tell me about) and a topic, but zero context — who is asking? what industry? what they already know? what problem are they solving? Without context, the AI produces a textbook-level generic answer. The Action (B) is present but severely underdeveloped; Context is the most critically missing element.

---

**Q15. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* Top-p (nucleus sampling) says: "Only consider tokens from the smallest set whose cumulative probability sums to at least p." At top-p = 0.9, only the most probable 90% of the probability mass is considered. This is an alternative to temperature for controlling output diversity without affecting the most-likely outputs.

---

**Q16. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* The key innovation of the Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017 — "Attention Is All You Need") is the self-attention mechanism, which allows the model to weigh the relationship between every token and every other token in the sequence simultaneously — unlike RNNs which processed sequentially (A). This enables both parallelization and much richer contextual understanding.

---

**Q17. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* When format matters — for professional use, copy-paste into documents, structured data extraction — always specify the exact output structure. "Respond in a markdown table with columns: [X, Y, Z]" or "Use numbered steps, each followed by a one-sentence explanation." This is consistently one of the highest-impact single changes you can make to a prompt.

---

**Q18. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* Option C contains Role (senior product manager at a B2B SaaS company), Context (team has asked for a PRD), and a specific Task (product requirements document for a new customer dashboard feature). Options A, B, and D are all vague — they give the AI no useful information to constrain its response.

---

**Q19. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* Token efficiency matters because API costs are priced per token (input + output). An efficient prompt achieves the same or better quality with fewer tokens — through precise language, removing redundant instructions, and not repeating context unnecessarily. More tokens do NOT automatically produce better results (C).

---

**Q20. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* Tone is most critical when the same information must be communicated differently to different audiences or in different emotional registers. A customer apology (empathetic, human) and an internal technical note (factual, direct) contain similar information but require completely different tones. For code (A) or data extraction (B), tone is largely irrelevant.

---

**Q21. Answer: D**
*Explanation:* "Explaining to a 10-year-old" is a Tone instruction — it defines the register, complexity level, and communication style. It is not Context (background information), Action (what to do), or Format (structure of the output). Tone shapes HOW the content is communicated.

---

**Q22. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* Prompt decomposition is the correct solution for complex tasks. Rather than overloading a single prompt (which dilutes attention and quality), breaking it into a sequence of focused prompts — each doing one thing well — produces dramatically better results. This is the foundation of multi-step pipelines covered in Module 5.

---

**Q23. Answer: C**
*Explanation:* Including a Role is a best practice — it is NOT a cause of failure. The three genuine failure causes listed (A, B, D) are: ambiguous task (the AI doesn't know what to produce), missing format (you get an unusable structure), and conflicting constraints (the AI cannot satisfy contradictory instructions and defaults to ignoring some).

---

**Q24. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* If Role, Context, Action, and Format are all correctly specified but the tone of outputs is consistently wrong — too formal, too casual, too technical — the missing element is always Tone (the T in CRAFT). Adding "Tone: empathetic and conversational, avoid jargon" as a specific Tone instruction directly targets this failure.

---

**Q25. Answer: B**
*Explanation:* All LLMs have a training data cutoff — a date after which they have no knowledge of world events. This is a critical limitation for professional use: never rely on an LLM for current events, recent data, or live information without providing that information in the prompt (RAG principle) or using a search-augmented model.

**B1.** Explain in your own words why the temperature parameter matters for professional AI use. Give one scenario where you would use low temperature and one where you would use high temperature. *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
Temperature controls the randomness of token selection during generation. At low temperature (0.0–0.3), the model consistently picks the most probable next token, producing predictable, accurate outputs — ideal for factual summaries, data extraction, classification, or code generation where consistency is critical. At high temperature (0.7–1.0), the model samples from a wider distribution, introducing creative variation — ideal for brainstorming, fiction writing, or generating multiple diverse alternatives to a marketing headline. Choosing the wrong temperature setting for the task is a common, easily-fixed prompt engineering error.

*Marking: 1 mark for correct explanation of temperature mechanics. 1 mark for two appropriate, clearly contrasted use cases.*

---

**B2.** A junior team member says: "The AI gave me a wrong answer, so AI tools are unreliable." How would you respond as a trained prompt engineer? *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
The quality of AI output is largely determined by the quality of the prompt — not just the model's inherent capability. A vague or incomplete prompt will reliably produce a generic or incorrect response; a precisely engineered prompt with Role, Context, Task, Format, and Tone will dramatically improve accuracy. Additionally, LLMs are text-prediction engines, not knowledge verification systems — all factual outputs must be independently verified before professional use. The correct mental model is: AI as a capable assistant who needs clear, specific instructions, not a magic answer machine.

*Marking: 1 mark for addressing prompt quality as the root cause. 1 mark for the verification/hallucination point.*

---

**B3.** What is the difference between the Context and the Role components in a CRAFT prompt? Give an example of each. *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
Role defines WHO the AI is being — the identity, expertise, or perspective it adopts (e.g., "You are a senior tax attorney with 15 years of corporate experience"). Context provides the background information ABOUT THE SITUATION the AI needs to give a relevant, tailored response (e.g., "My company is a 200-person SaaS startup exploring expansion into the EU market and we need to understand GDPR implications"). Role shapes the AI's perspective and expertise level; Context shapes the relevance and specificity of the content. Both are needed: Role without Context produces expert-sounding but generic advice; Context without Role produces relevant-topic but potentially shallow advice.

*Marking: 1 mark for clear conceptual distinction with definitions. 1 mark for a concrete example of each.*

---

**B4.** Describe three signs that a prompt needs to be revised based on its output. *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
Three clear revision signals: (1) **Generic output** — the AI's response could apply to any company or person in any context, suggesting the Context or Task is too vague. (2) **Wrong format** — the output is a paragraph when you needed a table, or a list when you needed a narrative, indicating a missing or unclear Format specification. (3) **Wrong tone or audience level** — the content is too technical for a non-technical audience or too casual for a board presentation, indicating a missing or insufficient Tone instruction. Additional valid signals include: hallucinated facts (needs RAG guardrails), missing information (needs more specific Task decomposition), or off-topic tangents (needs constraints).

*Marking: 1 mark for any 2 clearly described signals. 1 mark for all 3 with brief explanation of the root cause each reveals.*

---

**B5.** What is the practical significance of the context window limit for someone who wants to paste a long document into ChatGPT? *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
The context window is the total token budget shared between input and output. If you paste a very long document (e.g., a 50-page report), it may consume most or all of the context window — leaving little space for the model to generate a thorough response. More critically, if the document exceeds the context window entirely, the model either truncates the input (ignoring the excess) or returns an error. The practical solution is to extract only the relevant sections before pasting, use chunking strategies (summarize in sections, then synthesize the summaries), or use a model with a larger context window (e.g., Claude or Gemini with 200k+ tokens) for very long documents.

*Marking: 1 mark for explaining the token budget constraint. 1 mark for at least one practical workaround.*

**B6.** Write the CRAFT components for this task: "I need to write a rejection email for a job applicant who was a strong candidate but we hired someone with more specific experience." *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
- **C (Context):** We interviewed [Candidate Name] for the [Role] position. They were a strong candidate overall but we selected another applicant who had more direct experience in [specific area]. This person may apply again in future.
- **R (Role):** You are an HR professional writing on behalf of a company that values candidate relationships and employer brand.
- **A (Action):** Write a rejection email that thanks the candidate, acknowledges their strengths, explains the decision briefly without over-explaining, keeps the door open for future opportunities.
- **F (Format):** Standard email format. Subject line + body. Under 150 words.
- **T (Tone):** Warm, respectful, genuine. Not generic ("we received many strong applications"). Not false hope. Human.

*Marking: 1 mark for identifying all 5 CRAFT elements correctly. 1 mark for appropriately specific content in each element.*

---

**B7.** Why should a prompt engineer prefer "Write a 3-column table with columns: Benefit, Evidence, Source" over "Summarize the benefits"? *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
The first prompt specifies exact output format — a 3-column table with named columns — which constrains the AI to produce a structured, immediately usable output. The second prompt leaves format completely open, so the AI will default to its own judgment (likely prose paragraphs or unstructured bullets) which may require significant reformatting before use. In professional settings where outputs feed directly into documents, decks, or databases, format specification eliminates post-processing work and ensures consistency across multiple runs of the same prompt. Additionally, the first prompt implicitly asks for Evidence and Source — forcing the AI to support claims and flag what should be verified, improving output quality and accountability.

*Marking: 1 mark for the format control argument. 1 mark for noting the downstream usability and quality improvement.*

---

**B8.** What is the difference between a "topic label" slide headline and a "message headline"? Why does this matter for professional presentations? *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
A topic label simply names what a slide is about (e.g., "Revenue Growth" or "Market Analysis") — it gives the audience no information about what the takeaway is. A message headline states the specific finding or argument of that slide as a complete sentence (e.g., "Revenue grew 34% in Q3 driven by enterprise segment expansion" or "Our addressable market is 3x larger in Southeast Asia than in domestic markets"). Message headlines matter because: (1) audiences often scan slides before listening — message headlines communicate the conclusion even to someone who only reads headings; (2) they force the presenter to actually have a point on each slide rather than just a topic; (3) they align the visual and spoken message. This principle applies directly when prompting AI to generate presentation content.

*Marking: 1 mark for clear distinction with examples. 1 mark for the professional rationale.*

---

**B9.** A prompt engineer changes one constraint in a prompt and the output quality drops significantly. What should they do next and why? *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
They should revert the single change and test the original version again to confirm the change was the cause (not a random variation in model output). This is the "one change at a time" rule of prompt iteration — changing multiple elements simultaneously makes it impossible to identify which change caused the improvement or decline. Once the revert confirms the change was responsible for the drop, they should diagnose WHY that constraint was actually helping (perhaps it was providing implicit context, forcing a specific structure, or preventing a common failure mode) and then try a modified version that preserves the benefit differently. Prompt iteration is a diagnostic discipline, not a trial-and-error process.

*Marking: 1 mark for the revert-and-test protocol. 1 mark for the one-change-at-a-time rationale.*

---

**B10.** What are two things a prompt must do to minimize the risk of AI hallucination in professional outputs? *(2 marks)*

**Model Answer:**
(1) **Instruct the AI to flag uncertainty:** Include an instruction like "If you are uncertain about any fact, explicitly say so rather than stating it confidently" or "Add [VERIFY] next to any statistic or claim that should be independently confirmed." This converts silent hallucination into visible uncertainty that can be caught on review. (2) **Provide the source material in the prompt (RAG principle):** Instead of asking the AI to recall facts from training, paste the relevant document or data and instruct: "Answer using only the information provided. If the answer is not in the provided text, say so." This grounds the output in verifiable sources rather than statistical prediction. Additionally: always independently verify all factual claims, statistics, citations, and names before professional use — no prompt fully eliminates hallucination risk.

*Marking: 1 mark for each clearly explained strategy (2 marks total).*

**C1.** *(6 marks)* You are a data analyst at a retail company. Your manager has asked for a one-page summary of last quarter's performance for a non-technical audience (the leadership team). Write a COMPLETE CRAFT prompt that would generate a high-quality first draft of this summary. Then explain each CRAFT component you included and why you made those specific choices.

**Model Answer:**

**Complete CRAFT Prompt:**

```
You are a senior data analyst at a mid-size retail company with expertise in
translating complex performance data into executive-friendly narratives.

CONTEXT: The leadership team (CEO, CFO, COO, and 4 business unit heads) needs a
Q3 performance summary. They are not data professionals — they need clear, plain-
language narrative that highlights what happened, what it means, and what needs
attention. Q3 data: Revenue ₹42Cr (target ₹38Cr, +10.5% vs target, +18% YoY);
Gross margin 34% (vs 31% Q3 last year); Top-performing category: Electronics (+28%
YoY); Underperforming: Home Furnishings (-12% YoY); Customer returns increased 3%
vs Q2; eCommerce channel now 41% of total revenue (up from 31% last year).

TASK: Write a one-page (maximum 400 words) Q3 performance summary suitable for
presentation to the leadership team. Structure: (1) Opening headline — the single
most important Q3 finding in one sentence. (2) What went well — 3 highlights with
brief explanation of why they matter. (3) What needs attention — 2 challenges with
one-sentence implication each. (4) Forward-looking close — 1 sentence on what to
watch in Q4.

FORMAT: Flowing prose with a clear section structure. No tables. No jargon
(no "YoY," "basis points," or "EBITDA" — say "compared to last year," "margin
improvement," etc.). Headers for each section.

TONE: Confident, clear, and professionally optimistic. Acknowledge challenges
directly without alarm. Written as if the analyst is briefing the room — not
defensive, not padded with caveats.
```

**Explanation of Choices:**

- **Role:** Specifying "senior data analyst with expertise in executive narratives" calibrates the AI's language complexity and establishes the appropriate professional level. Without this, the AI might produce overly technical analysis.
- **Context:** The data is pasted directly into the prompt — this applies the RAG principle, grounding all output in verified numbers rather than invented figures. The audience description (non-technical, leadership) ensures the translation layer is applied.
- **Action:** The 4-part structure is explicitly specified to prevent the AI from deciding its own structure (which might not match what the leadership team expects from these reviews).
- **Format:** "Flowing prose" and "no jargon" directly target the most common failure mode for AI-generated data summaries — defaulting to bullet points and technical terms.
- **Tone:** "Professionally optimistic" and "not defensive" prevent another AI default — excessive hedging and caveat-loading — which undermines the confidence a leadership summary should project.

*Marking: 3 marks for complete, professionally sound CRAFT prompt. 3 marks for explanation — 1 mark per insightful component explanation (min 3 of 5).*

**C3.** *(6 marks)* Design a "Prompt Quality Scoring Rubric" for evaluating any CRAFT prompt before it is submitted. Your rubric should have at least 5 criteria, each scored 1–5 with clear definitions for each score level. Explain how this rubric would be used in a team setting.

**Model Answer:**

**Prompt Quality Rubric — CRAFT Standard**

| Criterion | 1 (Poor) | 3 (Adequate) | 5 (Excellent) |
|-----------|---------|-------------|--------------|
| **Role Clarity** | No role specified | Generic role ("you are an expert") | Specific role with domain, experience level, and perspective relevant to the task |
| **Context Completeness** | No context | Partial context — missing audience or purpose | Full context: who is the audience, what is the purpose, what is the setting, what does the AI need to know |
| **Task Specificity** | Vague task ("tell me about X") | Partially specific — topic clear but scope undefined | Specific task with defined scope, depth, required content elements, and any sub-structure |
| **Format Precision** | No format specified | Format named but not detailed ("use a table") | Format fully specified: structure type + column names / section names + length target |
| **Tone Appropriateness** | No tone or contradictory tone | Tone named but generic ("professional") | Tone calibrated to specific audience and purpose with concrete descriptors or anti-examples |
| **Constraint Clarity** | No constraints | 1–2 vague constraints | Clear positive and negative constraints; no conflicting instructions; edge cases addressed |

**Scoring:**
- **25–30:** Production-ready prompt
- **18–24:** Adequate for low-stakes tasks; refine before production
- **Below 18:** Redesign required before use

**Team Use:**
In a team setting, this rubric would be applied at two points: (1) **Before submission** — prompt author self-assesses and must reach ≥24 before submitting to the team library. This prevents low-quality prompts from becoming team standards. (2) **During peer review** — a second team member scores the prompt independently; any criterion where scores differ by ≥2 points triggers a discussion about what the expected standard is. This calibrates team-wide quality expectations over time and builds shared understanding of what "specific" and "precise" mean for their particular domain. The rubric also provides a vocabulary for prompt improvement: instead of "this prompt is bad," reviewers can say "the Task score is 2 — the scope is undefined."

*Marking: 3 marks for a complete, well-defined rubric (all 5 criteria with 3 score levels defined). 3 marks for practical team application explanation.*

**C4.** *(6 marks)* Write three versions of a prompt for the same task — one zero-elements prompt (just a question), one partial-CRAFT prompt (3 of 5 elements), and one full-CRAFT prompt (all 5 elements). Task: "Generate a competitive analysis of two companies." Show the expected quality difference in outputs and explain why each version produces what it does.

**Model Answer:**

**Version 1 — Zero Elements (Raw Question):**
```
Compare Apple and Google.
```
*Expected output:* A broad, generic comparison covering history, revenue, culture, products — whatever the model finds statistically most associated with "compare Apple and Google." No focus on competitive strategy, no specific audience, no format. Likely 400+ words of general information the user already knows.

*Why:* With no instructions beyond the subject, the AI optimizes for the most probable response to "compare X and Y" — which in its training data is a general overview covering multiple dimensions.

---

**Version 2 — Partial CRAFT (Role + Task + Format only):**
```
You are a business strategy analyst.

Compare Apple and Google's competitive positioning in the AI assistant market
(Siri vs. Google Assistant). Focus on: market share, key differentiators, and
strategic risks for each.

Respond in a table: Company | Market Share | Key Differentiator | Strategic Risk.
```
*Expected output:* Noticeably better — the Role elevates vocabulary and depth; the specific Task (AI assistant market, 3 specific dimensions) eliminates irrelevant content; the Format produces a directly usable comparison table. Missing: Context (who will read this? what decision does it inform?) and Tone.

*Why:* Three elements are enough to produce a usable output for simple tasks. The Role provides implicit tone. The specific Task limits scope. The Format structures output. What's missing is the "why" — without Context, the depth and angle may still be off.

---

**Version 3 — Full CRAFT:**
```
You are a senior competitive intelligence analyst specializing in the technology
sector, writing for a corporate strategy team.

CONTEXT: Our company is a mid-size enterprise software firm evaluating whether to
integrate an AI assistant feature into our product suite. We need to understand
how Apple and Google have positioned their AI assistants (Siri and Google
Assistant) in the enterprise market to inform our own go-to-market strategy.

TASK: Conduct a competitive analysis of Siri vs. Google Assistant specifically in
the B2B/enterprise market. Cover: (1) current enterprise adoption and market
position, (2) key product differentiators, (3) pricing/ecosystem strategy,
(4) documented weaknesses each has acknowledged, (5) strategic opportunity gaps
a new entrant could exploit.

FORMAT: Structured table for items 1–4 (one row per company). Item 5: 2–3 bullet
points below the table as a "Strategic Opportunity" section.

TONE: Analytical, evidence-based. Flag any claims that should be independently
verified with [VERIFY]. Do not pad with generic observations — every sentence
should inform a strategic decision.
```
*Expected output:* Highly targeted, enterprise-focused analysis with a specific audience (strategy team) and decision context (product integration), structured for immediate use in a strategy deck, with uncertainty flagged for verification.

*Why:* Every element does work: Role provides expertise calibration; Context defines the decision lens that shapes which information is relevant; Task specifies exact dimensions and their order; Format makes output directly usable; Tone prevents padding and flags verification needs.

**Quality Comparison:**

| Version | Relevance | Usability | Depth | Verification Safety |
|---------|-----------|-----------|-------|-------------------|
| Zero Elements | Low | Low | Superficial | Dangerous |
| Partial CRAFT | Medium | Medium | Moderate | Limited |
| Full CRAFT | High | High | Expert-level | Built-in |

*Marking: 2 marks for three clearly differentiated versions. 2 marks for accurate expected output descriptions. 2 marks for insightful explanation of why each produces what it does.*

**C5.** *(6 marks)* You are onboarding a new colleague who has never used AI tools professionally. Write a one-page "Prompt Engineering Quick-Start Guide" for them, covering: the most important concept, the 3 most common mistakes and how to fix them, and one complete example prompt with explanation. Use what you have learned in Module 1.

**Model Answer:**

---

**PROMPT ENGINEERING QUICK-START GUIDE**
*For new AI tool users — Module 1 summary*

**The Most Important Concept:**
The AI doesn't know anything about your situation until you tell it. Every prompt is a blank slate — the model has no knowledge of your role, your company, your audience, or your purpose. The quality of AI output is almost entirely determined by the quality of your instructions. Better prompt = better output. Every time.

---

**The 3 Most Common Mistakes (and Fixes):**

**Mistake 1: Being too vague**
❌ "Write something about our new product launch."
✅ Specify: who the audience is, what the product is, what the goal of the writing is, how long it should be.
Fix: Add at least 2 sentences of context before the task.

**Mistake 2: Not specifying output format**
❌ "Summarize the key points from this meeting."
✅ "Summarize the key points in a bulleted list: one bullet per decision made, one per action item (with owner and due date), one per open question."
Fix: Every time you want a specific structure, describe it explicitly.

**Mistake 3: Accepting the first output**
❌ "The AI gave me a bad answer — AI doesn't work."
✅ Read the output, diagnose what's wrong (wrong tone? wrong format? too generic?), change ONE thing in your prompt, and try again.
Fix: Treat prompt writing as editing — first draft is always a starting point.

---

**A Complete Example Prompt:**

```
You are a product marketing manager with experience writing for technical and
non-technical audiences.

CONTEXT: Our company is launching a new project management tool aimed at small
business owners (5–20 employees) who currently use spreadsheets. They are not
technical but are time-poor and results-focused.

TASK: Write a 3-sentence value proposition for our homepage hero section that
explains what the product does, who it's for, and why they should switch
from spreadsheets.

FORMAT: 3 sentences only. No bullet points. Plain English.

TONE: Confident, direct, benefit-first. No jargon. Speak to the reader's
frustration with spreadsheets, not our product's features.
```

**Why this works:**
- **Role** calibrates expertise (product marketing — knows both technical and non-technical communication)
- **Context** defines the audience precisely (small business owners, spreadsheet users, time-poor)
- **Task** is specific — 3 sentences, clear content requirements
- **Format** prevents the AI from writing an essay
- **Tone** prevents the most common marketing writing failure (feature-first instead of benefit-first)

---

*Remember: Every time AI output disappoints you, look at your prompt first — not the model.*
