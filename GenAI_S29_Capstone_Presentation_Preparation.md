## 1. The Presentation Challenge

### 1.1 What Makes a Technical Presentation Fail

```
THE MOST COMMON CAPSTONE PRESENTATION FAILURES:

FAILURE 1: "Features tour" instead of "problem-solution story"
  The presenter describes what each slide contains rather than
  telling a clear story of: problem → insight → solution → results.

FAILURE 2: Too much on every slide
  Bullet points covering every detail. Audience reads the slide,
  stops listening to the presenter.
  Fix: One idea per slide. Slide supports the speaker — not vice versa.

FAILURE 3: Missing the "So what?"
  The presenter explains what their solution does but never clearly
  states the business value. The evaluator is left asking: "So...why?"
  Fix: State the business impact explicitly before the demo.

FAILURE 4: Weak demo
  Demo is rushed, mumbled, or fails live.
  Fix: Rehearse the demo 5+ times. Have pre-run outputs as backup.

FAILURE 5: No story arc
  The presentation feels like a list of facts, not a narrative.
  Fix: Apply the SCR framework: Situation → Complication → Resolution.

FAILURE 6: Robotic delivery
  The presenter reads from the slides word-for-word.
  Fix: Slides show key points; speaker notes guide natural elaboration.
```

## 2. The Presentation Structure: 10 Slides

### 2.1 The 10-Slide Capstone Deck

```
SLIDE 1 — TITLE SLIDE:
  Project name, your name, date
  Subtitle: 1 sentence describing the solution
  Optional: A strong visual that hints at the theme

SLIDE 2 — THE PROBLEM:
  Title: [Assertion — state the problem's impact]
  Content: Who has this problem + what is the cost/impact (quantified)
  Visual: A single striking statistic or a "before" scenario description

SLIDE 3 — THE ROOT CAUSE:
  Title: [Why the problem exists — the insight]
  Content: The root cause you identified (from Problem Definition Canvas)
  Why this insight matters: most solutions address the symptom

SLIDE 4 — THE SOLUTION CONCEPT:
  Title: [What you built — in one assertion]
  Content: 3-sentence description of what the solution is and does
  Visual: A simple pipeline diagram or the solution name/logo

SLIDE 5 — HOW IT WORKS:
  Title: [The process — simply stated]
  Content: Your pipeline/workflow (4–5 steps, visual)
  One column: Input → Steps → Output
  Keep it simple — this is for understanding, not technical review

SLIDE 6 — LIVE DEMONSTRATION:
  Title: "Live Demonstration — [Core Capability]"
  Content: Placeholder for live demo context
  [Describe what you will demonstrate — shown on the slide before you demo]
  Visual: Screenshot of your demo interface or first sample output

SLIDE 7 — RESULTS AND EVIDENCE:
  Title: [Lead with your strongest result]
  Content: Your 3 success metrics with before/after evidence
  Visual: Simple comparison table or before/after graphic

SLIDE 8 — BUSINESS IMPACT:
  Title: [ROI statement — annual or monthly]
  Content: Extrapolate from your test results:
  "At [X frequency], this solution saves [Y hours] per [period]
   at an equivalent cost saving of [₹ estimate]"
  Responsible AI: Note the human oversight built in

SLIDE 9 — LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS:
  Title: [What this solution currently doesn't do]
  Content: 2–3 current limitations (honest) + 2–3 next development steps
  Shows: Professional self-awareness and forward thinking

SLIDE 10 — SUMMARY AND CLOSE:
  Title: [One-sentence summary of the value delivered]
  Content: 3 key takeaways (one from problem, one from solution, one from results)
  Close: Thank you + your name + contact + invitation for Q&A
```

### 2.2 Assertion-Based Slide Titles

The title of every slide must communicate the main point — not just label the topic.

```
WEAK (LABEL): "Problem Overview"
STRONG (ASSERTION): "Marketing teams spend 45 minutes on every email — 
                     40 of which are recoverable with AI"

WEAK (LABEL): "Solution Description"
STRONG (ASSERTION): "A 5-prompt AI system that takes input to publish-ready 
                     output in under 10 minutes"

WEAK (LABEL): "Results"
STRONG (ASSERTION): "Response time reduced 83%; quality scores unchanged"

WEAK (LABEL): "Limitations"
STRONG (ASSERTION): "Current version handles 80% of use cases — 
                     3 extensions will close the gap"
```

## 3. Using AI to Build the Presentation

### 3.1 Presentation Outline Generator

```
PROMPT:
You are a professional presentation coach helping me prepare a 10-minute
capstone presentation for my AI certification program.

My solution summary:
  Problem: [PASTE YOUR PROBLEM STATEMENT]
  Solution: [PASTE YOUR SOLUTION DESCRIPTION]
  Key results: [PASTE YOUR 3 SUCCESS METRICS WITH EVIDENCE]
  Category: [A/B/C/D]

Using the SCR framework (Situation → Complication → Resolution):
  SITUATION: What is the current state of affairs?
  COMPLICATION: What goes wrong / what is the problem?
  RESOLUTION: How does my solution resolve the complication?

Generate:
1. A complete 10-slide structure following the prescribed format
2. For each slide: an assertion-based title + 3-bullet content guide
3. The SCR thread — show how the narrative flows from slide to slide
4. A suggested opening line (first sentence of the presentation — not
   "Hi my name is..." but an arresting hook that starts with the problem)
5. A suggested closing line (last sentence before Q&A invitation)
```

### 3.2 Speaker Notes Generator

```
PROMPT:
Write speaker notes for this presentation slide.

SLIDE NUMBER: [X of 10]
SLIDE TITLE: [YOUR ASSERTION TITLE]
SLIDE CONTENT: [BULLET POINTS ON THE SLIDE]
TIME ALLOCATED: [X seconds/minutes for this slide]
PRECEDING SLIDE TOPIC: [What came before]
FOLLOWING SLIDE TOPIC: [What comes after]

Write speaker notes that:
- Open with a TRANSITION from the previous slide (1 sentence linking the two)
- Expand on the slide content with 2–3 sentences of natural spoken explanation
- Include a SPECIFIC EXAMPLE or EVIDENCE for the key point
- Close with a BRIDGE SENTENCE to the next slide

Format: Written as if speaking naturally — not bullet points.
Approximately [WORD COUNT: time × 130 words/minute] words.
No jargon. No reading from the slide. The notes elaborate, not repeat.
```

### 3.3 Tough Q&A Preparation

```
PROMPT:
I am presenting an AI capstone project to a panel of evaluators.
Here is my solution:
[PASTE YOUR SOLUTION ONE-PAGER]

Generate the 5 toughest questions an evaluator could ask me,
including skeptics and critics of AI solutions.

For each question:
Q: [THE TOUGH QUESTION]
WHY THEY'RE ASKING IT: [What concern or doubt is behind this question]
STRONG ANSWER STRUCTURE:
  DIRECT ANSWER: [Yes/No/Qualified response — 1 sentence]
  EVIDENCE: [What you can point to from your testing / design]
  ACKNOWLEDGMENT: [What the question gets right — showing you're not defensive]
  FORWARD VIEW: [What development would address the concern further]

Also include:
- 1 "gotcha" question about responsible AI
- 1 question testing whether you understand the limitations of your solution
- 1 question about how you would scale this beyond your test case
```

## 4. The 10-Minute Delivery Plan

### 4.1 Time Allocation

```
10-MINUTE PRESENTATION TIME ALLOCATION:

Slide 1 (Title): 20 seconds — opening hook, not "hello my name is..."
Slide 2 (Problem): 60 seconds — make the audience feel the pain
Slide 3 (Root Cause): 45 seconds — the insight that makes your solution smart
Slide 4 (Solution Concept): 30 seconds — brief, then save detail for slide 5
Slide 5 (How It Works): 60 seconds — walk through the pipeline simply
Slide 6 (Demo): 180 seconds — the heart of the presentation (3 minutes)
Slide 7 (Results): 60 seconds — your evidence, specific and confident
Slide 8 (Business Impact): 30 seconds — the so-what in financial terms
Slide 9 (Limitations & Next Steps): 30 seconds — professional and honest
Slide 10 (Summary): 25 seconds — one crisp wrap-up + Q&A invitation

TOTAL: ~10 minutes (600 seconds)

TIME RISK AREAS:
  Demo: Easiest to overrun. Practice ending exactly on time.
  Problem: Easiest to rush. The problem must land before the solution matters.
  Q&A: Typically 5 minutes after the 10-minute presentation.
```

### 4.2 The Opening Hook

The first 20 seconds determine whether the audience is engaged. Never open with:
- "Hi, my name is..."
- "Today I'm going to be presenting..."
- "So, for my capstone project, I decided to..."

**Open with the problem — make it immediate and real:**

```
OPENING HOOK FORMATS:

FORMAT 1 — THE STATISTIC:
"Every HR manager in a mid-sized company spends 40 hours a year writing
performance reviews. Most of that time produces generic, forgettable
feedback. I built a solution that changes that."

FORMAT 2 — THE SCENARIO:
"It's 10 PM on a Friday. A manager has 6 performance reviews due Monday.
She opens a blank document and stares at it for 20 minutes.
This happens in almost every company, every quarter. I want to show you
what happens when AI steps in at that moment."

FORMAT 3 — THE QUESTION:
"How long does it take your team to produce one week of social media content?
If the answer is more than 3 hours — by the end of this presentation,
I'd like to change that number."
```

### 4.3 The Demo Script

The live demonstration is the most important and most risky part. Script it:

```
DEMO SCRIPT TEMPLATE:

[TRANSITION INTO DEMO]
"Let me show you this working. I'm going to use a real input from
[context — a scenario your audience will recognize]."

[SHOW THE INPUT]
"Here's what the user — in this case, a [ROLE] — would enter:
[READ KEY PARTS OF YOUR INPUT ALOUD — don't assume they can read it]"

[RUN THE DEMO]
"I'm running this now... [wait for output]"

[WALK THROUGH THE OUTPUT]
"Notice: [POINT 1 — the most important thing the output does well]
And here: [POINT 2 — the second most impressive element]
This section: [POINT 3 — address the problem's root cause directly]"

[CONNECT TO THE RESULT]
"This output took [X seconds/minutes]. Doing this manually took [Y].
The manager now reviews, makes any edits — typically [Z] — and submits."

[TRANSITION OUT]
"Let me show you what that means for the results."
```

  minimal text per slide (max 5 short bullets or 1 main statement).

## 6. Presentation Confidence Techniques

### 6.1 The Three Rehearsal Runs

```
RUN 1 — STRUCTURE RUN (alone, out loud):
  Walk through your full deck speaking aloud.
  Don't worry about polish — just confirm the story flows.
  Time it: Are you within 10 minutes?
  Identify: Which slides take too long? Which transition awkwardly?

RUN 2 — DEMO RUN (with demo, out loud):
  Full presentation including the live demo.
  Time the demo segment specifically.
  Practice your demo script — same words every time.
  Test: Does your technology work? Do you have your pre-run backup?

RUN 3 — FULL RUN (with a colleague or friend as audience):
  Ask them to play the evaluator.
  Give them your 5 tough Q&As — ask them to challenge you.
  Time the full presentation + Q&A.
  Debrief: What was unclear? What was compelling?
```

### 6.2 Managing Nerves

```
PREPARATION-BASED CONFIDENCE:
  ✓ Know your content cold (this is the only real cure for nerves)
  ✓ Know your demo will work (pre-run backup means zero risk)
  ✓ Know your Q&A answers (prepared → confident → calm)

DELIVERY TECHNIQUES:
  ✓ Speak slower than you think you need to
  ✓ Pause after key statements — let them land
  ✓ Make eye contact (or camera contact in virtual settings)
  ✓ If something goes wrong: say "Let me move to my pre-run output"
     (having a backup is the professional move — use it confidently)

REFRAMING NERVOUSNESS:
  Nervous energy is physiologically identical to excited energy.
  Tell yourself: "I'm excited to show what I built" — not "I'm nervous."
  The physical response is the same. The mental frame is different.
```

## 9. Interview Questions — Session 29

**Q1:** *"How do you present technical work to a non-technical audience?"*

**Strong Answer:**
"I follow the SCR narrative framework: Situation → Complication → Resolution. I open with the problem in terms the audience already feels — not with technical details of my solution — because the problem is what creates the need for what I'm showing them. I use assertion-based slide titles so every slide communicates its point before I speak a word. For technical demonstrations, I script the walkthrough precisely and practice it until the demo takes exactly the time I planned for it — I also always prepare pre-run backup outputs so a live connection issue doesn't derail the evidence. For Q&A, I prepare structured answers to the 5 toughest questions in advance: a direct answer, evidence from testing, acknowledgment of what the question gets right, and a forward view showing I know what development is still needed. The goal is that someone who knows nothing about AI walks away understanding the business problem I solved and trusting the evidence I provided."

## 11. Key Terminology — Session 29

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **SCR Framework** | Situation, Complication, Resolution — a narrative structure for presenting a problem and solution |
| **Assertion Title** | A slide title that states the main point or finding, rather than just labeling the slide's topic |
| **Speaker Notes** | Written elaboration for each slide used to guide natural, spoken delivery |
| **Demo Script** | A word-for-word scripted walkthrough of the live demonstration component |
| **Opening Hook** | The first sentence of a presentation — designed to immediately engage the audience with the problem |
| **Pre-Run Output** | A saved copy of demonstration output prepared in advance as a backup for live demo failures |
| **Q&A Preparation** | Pre-prepared structured answers to anticipated tough evaluator questions |
| **Gamma.app** | An AI presentation tool that generates designed slide decks from text outlines |
