## 29.1 The Capstone Presentation Brief

Your capstone presentation has a specific structure, audience, and purpose:

```
PRESENTATION BRIEF:
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Duration: 10 minutes presentation + 10 minutes Q&A
Audience: [Instructor + peer cohort] — all have completed the same course;
          they understand the techniques but have not seen your project
Purpose: Demonstrate that you can apply AI prompt engineering to solve
         a real professional problem and communicate the results clearly
Format: Slides + spoken delivery; no reading from notes
Assessment: 10 points in rubric for presentation quality

THE STORY YOU ARE TELLING:
"Here was a real problem. Here is how I used what I learned to solve it.
Here is the evidence it worked. Here is what you could take away from my approach."

YOUR AUDIENCE WANTS TO KNOW:
1. Is this a real, meaningful problem? (not a trivial demo)
2. Did you actually apply the techniques (not just talk about them)?
3. Can I see evidence it worked? (before/after, quality scores, time data)
4. What can I learn or steal from your approach?
5. Are you a credible, confident presenter of your own work?
```

## 29.3 Generating Slide Content with AI

Use the slide content generation techniques from Session 13 to build each slide.

### Capstone Presentation Content Prompt

```
You are an executive presentation coach helping me build my capstone
presentation for a prompt engineering certification program.

My project summary:
- Problem solved: [DESCRIBE]
- Solution built: [DESCRIBE — techniques, pipeline, library size]
- Impact data: [PASTE YOUR BEFORE/AFTER TABLE]
- Key lesson: [WHAT DID YOU LEARN THAT SURPRISED YOU?]

Build my capstone presentation following this 10-slide structure:
[PASTE SLIDE MAP FROM SECTION 29.2]

For each slide:
MESSAGE HEADLINE: [Complete sentence stating the takeaway — not a topic label]
BULLETS: [Maximum 3, maximum 8 words each]
VISUAL DESCRIPTION: [What image, diagram, or chart would work best]
SPEAKER NOTE: [2–3 sentences — conversational, not read-aloud text]
TRANSITION TO NEXT SLIDE: [The sentence connecting this slide to the next]

Principles:
- Every headline is a complete sentence with a specific claim
- No slide has more than 3 bullets
- Every slide advances the narrative — not just adds more information
- The story arc: Problem → Solution → Evidence → Lesson → Action

Tone: Confident practitioner sharing real work. Not academic. Not sales pitch.
Human, specific, evidence-based.
```

### The Live Demo Moment (Slide 4)

For maximum impact, include a 30-second live demonstration in Slide 4.

```
LIVE DEMO PREPARATION:

Choose: The single most impressive thing your system does.
Not the most technically complex — the most VISUALLY compelling.
Example: Taking 3 lines of raw notes → a complete, polished email in 15 seconds.

Prepare:
□ Sample input ready to paste (on your laptop, not to type live)
□ AI tool open with system prompt pre-loaded
□ Backup slide: "If live demo fails — here's the recorded output"
  (Screenshot of the demo running, as a fallback)

Script for demo moment (30 seconds):
"Here's [describe the input — raw, messy, simple].
[Paste input]
Here's what my system produces in [time]:
[AI generates]
[Point out 2 specific quality elements]
This used to take me [baseline time]. Now it takes [after time]."
```

## 29.4 The Reflection Essay

The 500-word reflection essay is a required deliverable. It is assessed for depth of insight, intellectual honesty, and evidence of genuine mindset change.

### Reflection Essay Structure and Prompt

```
Write my 500-word capstone reflection essay.

Reflection prompt: "How has your approach to using AI changed from
Session 1 to Session 30? What is the most important mindset shift
you experienced?"

My reflection notes (help me expand these into a structured essay):

Where I started:
- My initial view of AI at Session 1: [HONEST DESCRIPTION — skeptic? overwhelmed? overconfident?]
- My biggest misconception at the start: [WHAT DID YOU GET WRONG?]

What changed:
- The session that changed my thinking most: [WHICH ONE AND WHY?]
- The technique I expected to be simple but wasn't: [BE SPECIFIC]
- The failure that taught me the most: [DESCRIBE A SPECIFIC PROMPT FAILURE]
- The moment I felt the power of AI productivity for real: [SPECIFIC MOMENT]

The capstone experience:
- What surprised me most about building my project: [HONEST]
- The hardest part was: [SPECIFIC CHALLENGE]
- The result I'm most proud of: [SPECIFIC]

Looking forward:
- The most important habit I will maintain: [SPECIFIC, NOT GENERIC]
- What I will build next: [CONCRETE NEXT PROJECT OR GOAL]
- What I would tell a colleague starting this program: [YOUR BEST ADVICE]

Essay requirements:
- 500 words (±50)
- First person throughout
- Specific — not generic platitudes about AI being powerful
- Honest — include what didn't go as planned
- Forward-looking conclusion
- No buzzwords
```

## 29.5 Q&A Preparation

Using the Q&A preparation technique from Session 13, anticipate and prepare for the hardest questions your panel will ask.

### Capstone Q&A Preparation Prompt

```
Generate the 10 hardest questions my presentation panel is likely to ask
about my capstone project.

My project summary:
- Track: [A/B/C/D]
- Problem: [DESCRIBE]
- Solution: [DESCRIBE]
- Key claim: [YOUR MAIN IMPACT CLAIM]
- Techniques used: [LIST]
- Limitations I know about: [BE HONEST]

For each question:
1. The question (phrased as a skeptical but fair assessor would ask it)
2. What this question is really testing (what concern underlies it)
3. My recommended answer strategy (acknowledge / reframe / provide data)
4. A suggested response (3–4 sentences — confident, honest, specific)
5. What NOT to say (the defensive or evasive answer that would cost marks)

Question categories to cover:
- Methodology questions (why did you choose this technique over others?)
- Evidence questions (how do you know this is better than your baseline?)
- Scalability questions (would this work for a larger team?)
- Responsible AI questions (what bias did you find and what did you do?)
- Edge case questions (what happens when your prompts fail?)
- Next steps questions (what would you build next?)
- Generalizability questions (what can others apply from your work?)
```

## Hands-On Activities — Session 29

---

### Activity 29.1 — Build All 10 Slides

Using the Capstone Presentation Content Prompt (Section 29.3), generate content for all 10 slides.

For each slide: verify the message headline is a complete sentence with a specific claim (not a topic label). Revise any that are still topic labels.

Apply the 3-bullet maximum and 8-word maximum per bullet rules.

---

### Activity 29.2 — Write the Reflection Essay

Use the Reflection Essay Prompt (Section 29.4) — but fill in your own honest reflections before running the prompt. The AI expands and polishes; your genuine insight must come first.

After generating the draft: read it aloud. Does it sound like you? If it sounds like a generic AI essay about AI, revise with more specific personal details.

---

### Activity 29.3 — Q&A Preparation Drill

Run the Q&A Preparation Prompt. Then:
- Print or write the 10 questions
- Answer each one OUT LOUD (not in writing) — time yourself
- For the 3 you feel least confident about, ask AI for deeper preparation
- Practice with a colleague if possible: they ask questions, you answer

---

### Activity 29.4 — Self-Assessment

Run the Self-Assessment Prompt. Be ruthless with your scoring.

For any section scoring below 70% of its maximum: make specific improvements before Session 30. You have one more session — use the time on the highest-value improvements.

---

### Activity 29.5 — Organize Submission Package

Create the folder structure from Section 29.7. Move all deliverables into the correct folders. Write the README. Verify every required deliverable is present.

Have someone else open your submission package cold and tell you: "Can you find [specific deliverable] in under 30 seconds?" If not, reorganize.
