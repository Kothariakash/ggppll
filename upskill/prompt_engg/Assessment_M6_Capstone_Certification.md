# PART A — ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

**Q1. Answer: B** — The Capstone demonstrates applied capability. Its value comes from building a real solution to a genuine professional problem — not theoretical knowledge or technical complexity. The final deliverable proves the student can independently design, build, test, and evaluate an AI solution.

**Q2. Answer: B** — A strong Problem Definition is specific and quantifiable: "Marketing analysts spend 6 hours/week classifying customer feedback — a task with 15% error rate that delays weekly reporting by 2 days." Vague definitions produce poorly scoped projects that cannot demonstrate measurable improvement.

**Q3. Answer: B** — Baseline measurement is the foundation of demonstrable ROI. Without a genuine pre-build baseline, the Before/After comparison is fabricated. This measurement must happen before the first Capstone prompt is written.

**Q4. Answer: B** — A quality gate is an evaluation checkpoint: "Before building prompts 6–10, verify that prompts 1–5 each score ≥4/5 on the quality rubric." Gates prevent building on a weak foundation.

**Q5. Answer: B** — The 10-slide architecture is a completeness framework ensuring every key dimension is communicated: problem, evidence, solution, results, responsibility, and future. It is not a rigid slide count rule.

**Q6. Answer: B** — The Reflection Essay is the highest-order learning evidence — it demonstrates metacognition. Assessors look for: honest acknowledgment of failures, specific insights from experience, and concrete "what I'd do differently" thinking. A reflection that only describes successes is not a reflection.

**Q7. Answer: C** — Track C (Business Function AI Workflow) is designed for building comprehensive AI systems for a specific business function — exactly what a content marketing workflow system requires.

**Q8. Answer: B** — Adversarial Q&A preparation generates the hardest questions about YOUR specific project — surfacing specific vulnerabilities in your approach, data choices, and Responsible AI decisions. Generic AI questions don't expose your project's specific weaknesses.

**Q9. Answer: B** — Responsible AI Assessment is a systematic audit of the complete solution across all four pillars: Fairness (bias), Transparency (disclosure), Accountability (human review), and Privacy (data handling). Assessors look for genuine engagement with specific findings, not generic declarations.

**Q10. Answer: B** — The Before/After comparison requires at least one quantified dimension. "It feels faster" is not evidence. "Time reduced from 3 hours to 28 minutes over 4 measured weeks" is evidence.

**Q11. Answer: B** — Peer review develops a professional skill used throughout careers: giving structured, specific, actionable feedback and receiving it constructively. The Capstone session is a low-stakes environment to develop this high-stakes capability.

**Q12. Answer: B** — A specific, relatable problem creates immediate relevance. "Every week your analysts spend 4 hours on a report that takes 10 minutes to read" lands before any AI is mentioned. A definition or demo before the audience understands why it matters fails to create engagement.

**Q13. Answer: B** — The complete deliverables package is a professional portfolio: Problem Definition (scope + baseline), prompts with metadata (the system), demonstration outputs (evidence it works), Before/After (evidence of value), Responsible AI (evidence of ethical practice), and Reflection Essay (evidence of learning depth).

**Q14. Answer: B** — The Post-Certification Plan prevents the common pattern of skills atrophying after a program ends. A concrete 90-day roadmap with specific actions, projects, and communities maintains momentum.

**Q15. Answer: B** — "Treat every prompt as an experiment — design, test, diagnose, and refine systematically" is a core program principle. Iteration is the method; random re-running is not iteration.

**Q16. Answer: B** — Showing the prompt alongside the output proves the result is the product of skill, not luck. It makes prompt engineering visible and demonstrates repeatability — the two things evaluators need to see.

**Q17. Answer: B** — A documented library with metadata, version history, and performance notes is a professional asset. An undocumented folder of prompt text files is not a library — it is an archive with no reuse value.

**Q18. Answer: B** — Discovering a bias issue during auditing is the audit working correctly. Documenting the finding, implementing mitigation, testing effectiveness, and including the process in the Responsible AI section demonstrates more sophisticated ethical practice than a project claiming no issues.

**Q19. Answer: B** — Baseline measurement must happen before any AI work begins. Post-build baseline estimation is subject to memory bias and undermines the credibility of all ROI claims.

**Q20. Answer: B** — A focused, well-executed 10-prompt system demonstrates depth: quality, measurable improvement, full documentation, and responsible AI assessment. Breadth without depth demonstrates activity, not capability.

**Q21. Answer: B** — The Scaling Plan shows the evaluator the student understands their project as a prototype. "This could serve all 8 analysts within 60 days" or "API integration would eliminate manual copy-paste" demonstrates strategic thinking beyond the immediate build.

**Q22. Answer: C** — Intellectual honesty is a professional strength. "I don't have enough data to answer that confidently — here's what I'd investigate" is credible and demonstrates competence. Speculation risks a confidently wrong answer that destroys credibility.

**Q23. Answer: B** — Certification requires both: knowledge (≥70% on written assessments) and applied capability (PASS on Capstone rubric). Either alone is insufficient.

**Q24. Answer: A** — Quality, real-world relevance, and completeness determine Capstone achievement. A focused solution with quantified improvement, full documentation, and genuine Responsible AI engagement demonstrates the complete capability set the program develops.

**Q25. Answer: B** — A specific Call to Action closes with exactly what you want the audience to do: "I'm proposing we pilot this with the analytics team for 30 days — I need access, a point of contact, and 30 minutes of their time per week." Vague closes leave audiences without direction.

**B1.** What distinguishes a Capstone project scope that will succeed from one that will fail? Give one example of each. *(2 marks)*

**Answer:** A successful scope is: specific (one well-defined task), achievable (5–10 core prompts), measurable (clear Before/After comparison), and genuinely useful (solves a real problem). A failing scope is: too broad (automating an entire department), vague ("use AI to improve marketing"), unmeasurable (no baseline), or purely theoretical.

**Successful:** "Build a 6-prompt pipeline classifying weekly NPS survey responses — reducing analyst processing from 4 hours to under 30 minutes."
**Failing:** "Build an AI system that transforms our company's entire customer experience strategy."

The second is unachievable in the timeframe, unmeasurable, and has no clear starting point.

**B4.** Write the 10 Principles of Professional Prompt Engineering a certified graduate must articulate. *(2 marks)*

**Answer:**
1. **Specificity beats length** — A precise, short prompt outperforms a long, vague one.
2. **Context enables relevance** — Tell the AI who, what, for whom, and why — every time.
3. **Format drives usability** — Wrong format = wasted output. Specify structure before content.
4. **Every prompt is an experiment** — Design, test, diagnose, change one thing, repeat.
5. **Role calibrates quality** — "Expert" is not a role. Assign specific domain, level, perspective.
6. **Decompose complexity** — One task per prompt. Chain focused steps.
7. **Verify everything** — No AI output is automatically true. Every fact needs independent verification.
8. **The professional is always accountable** — Responsibility cannot be delegated to a tool.
9. **Build systems, not one-off prompts** — Documented, versioned libraries compound in value.
10. **Responsible use is non-negotiable** — Fairness, transparency, accountability, and privacy are requirements, not aspirations.

*Marking: 1 mark for any 7 correctly stated. 1 mark for all 10 clearly articulated.*

**B5.** What makes a Capstone demonstration effective vs. merely showing outputs? *(2 marks)*

**Answer:** An effective demonstration makes prompt engineering skill visible in three ways: (1) **Show prompt alongside output** — the audience sees both simultaneously, understanding that the quality of the result is the product of specific engineering choices. This proves repeatability and skill. (2) **Show a Before/After pair** — what does an unengineered prompt produce vs. your engineered prompt? The contrast demonstrates the VALUE of prompt engineering itself. (3) **Narrate design decisions** — explain WHY each element was included: "I added this negative instruction because the AI defaulted to bullet points, which didn't match the executive narrative format." Narrating decisions demonstrates understanding, not just execution. Showing only impressive outputs is a magic trick — it impresses but proves nothing about skill.

*Marking: 1 mark for the show-prompt-alongside-output point. 1 mark for Before/After and narrated decisions.*

**B8.** How should a presenter respond to: "Can you guarantee your AI solution won't produce biased outputs?" *(2 marks)*

**Answer:** This cannot and should not be answered with "yes." The correct four-part response: **(1) Direct honesty:** "No — and I wouldn't claim that. Bias in AI systems is a documented, real risk that cannot be completely eliminated." **(2) What was done:** "My system includes a bias audit sub-prompt that checks every output against 6 documented bias patterns. In 45 test cases, it caught 7 potential issues the first-draft prompt introduced." **(3) The safeguard:** "Every output is reviewed by an HR Business Partner before use. The AI is a drafting assistant, not a gatekeeper." **(4) Remaining honesty:** "What I cannot guarantee is that the audit catches everything — particularly novel bias patterns not in my test cases. This is why human review is non-negotiable in my design."

This response is more credible than a guarantee because intellectual honesty demonstrates genuine understanding of the problem.

*Marking: 1 mark for the four-part structure (don't guarantee + what you did + safeguard). 1 mark for the intellectual honesty rationale.*

**B9.** What must a Capstone prompt library contain to qualify as a professional deliverable? *(2 marks)*

**Answer:** A professional prompt library is not a folder of text files — it is a documented, governed asset. Each entry must contain: (1) **Unique ID** — for cross-referencing and version tracking. (2) **Task description** — what this prompt does in plain language, so users find it by need. (3) **Technique** — Zero-shot / Few-shot / CoT / Persona / Chain — so users understand the approach. (4) **Version number** — semantic versioning (v2.1.0) showing change history. (5) **Performance record** — success rate, tested inputs, known failure modes — the most decision-critical field. (6) **Iteration history** — what was tried, what failed, what was changed, and why — preventing others from repeating failed approaches. Additionally: a searchable index, folder structure by use case, and retirement criteria for outdated entries.

*Marking: 1 mark for listing 5+ required fields. 1 mark for the distinction between a library (governed, documented) vs. a folder of prompts.*

**C2.** *(6 marks)* Design the Build Phase for the Capstone project in C1 — show 5 prompts with CRAFT structure, the quality gate between Prompts 2 and 3, and a measurement framework.

**Answer:**

**Prompt 1 — Incident Summarization:**
```
ROLE: Senior IT Operations analyst writing for non-technical leadership
CONTEXT: Incident Summary section, Weekly Operations Review. Audience: CEO, CFO, COO
— they care about business impact, not technical detail.
TASK: From the incident log below, summarize P1/P2 incidents (system, business impact,
resolution time, repeat flag). P3+ incidents: count only + pattern note.
INPUT: {incident_log_export CSV}
FORMAT: Table for P1/P2 | P3+ count as sentence | Overall status: GREEN/AMBER/RED
TONE: Factual, no jargon, no acronyms unexpanded. Leadership-ready.
Do not add interpretations not supported by the data.
```

**Prompt 2 — Performance Narrative:**
```
ROLE: Senior operations manager writing system performance narrative
CONTEXT: Performance section for COO and leadership. Non-technical audience — they
need to understand whether systems met expectations and what deviations mean.
TASK: Write performance narrative for this week.
INPUT: {dashboard_export: uptime %, response times, error rates vs. SLA}
FORMAT: Flowing narrative 150–200 words. No bullet points. Numbers always in
context ("99.2% — 0.3% below our 99.5% SLA target", not just "99.2%").
TONE: Confident and specific. Name underperforming systems explicitly.
```

**Prompt 3 — Capacity Update:**
```
ROLE: Operations Manager writing team capacity section
CONTEXT: Leadership uses this to make project commitments and deadline decisions.
TASK: Write Capacity Update for this week.
INPUT: {team_status: headcount, projects, upcoming leaves, open positions}
FORMAT: 3–4 short paragraphs. Max 150 words.
TONE: Honest and specific. Do not soften a constraint — leadership needs the real picture.
Include: current capacity %, constraints in next 2 weeks, open position impact,
recommendation: can team absorb new work this sprint?
```

**Prompt 4 — Risk Flag Generation:**
```
ROLE: Senior Operations Risk Analyst
CONTEXT: Risk Flags section — proactive identification of next-week risks.
TASK: From this week's operations context, identify 3–5 risk flags for next week.
INPUT: {Incident summary + Performance narrative + Capacity update + forward_calendar}
FORMAT: Numbered list. Per flag: risk name | why it's a risk (from THIS week's data)
| recommended action | likelihood: HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW
Do not generate risks not supported by this week's data. Low-confidence flags:
note what additional data would clarify.
```

**Prompt 5 — Report Assembly:**
```
ROLE: Document formatter preparing executive distribution
TASK: Combine these 4 sections into a complete, consistently formatted Operations Review.
INPUTS: {Sections 1–4 outputs}
FORMAT: Title: "Weekly Operations Review — Week of {DATE}" | Bold section headers |
Page break before Risk Flags | Footer with date and distribution list
Consistency audit: flag any terminology inconsistency across sections with [REVIEW]
Do not change substantive content — format and consistency only.
```

**Quality Gate — Between Prompts 2 and 3:**

Before running Prompt 3, Prompt 2 output must pass:
- [ ] Headline names specific systems (not "overall performance was good")
- [ ] All numbers presented with SLA context, not raw values
- [ ] Below-SLA systems specifically named and explained
- [ ] Word count 150–200
- [ ] Ends with forward-looking statement

**Gate:** Must pass 4/5. Fail → diagnose → revise Prompt 2 specifically → re-run. Do not proceed to Prompt 3 until passed.

**Why this gate:** Performance narrative feeds into Prompt 4 (Risk Flags). A vague Performance section produces vague risks — weak Section 2 contaminates Section 4 through the context chain.

**Measurement Framework:**

| Metric | Method | Target |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Time per section | Stopwatch, logged | Prompts 1–4: ≤5 min each |
| Quality score | Manager rates 1–5 per rubric | ≥4.0/5 average |
| Revision rate | % needing >1 round | <30% |
| Accuracy | Spot-check 3 data points vs. source | 0 undetected errors |
| End-to-end time | Start to assembled report | ≤60 minutes |

*Marking: 3 marks for 5 complete CRAFT prompts. 2 marks for quality gate with specific criteria and propagation rationale. 1 mark for measurement framework.*
